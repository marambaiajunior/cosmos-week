Astrophysicists have recently observed a pulsar rotating over 300 times per second that appeared to be slowing down unusually quickly.

Researchers at the National Astronomical Observatories of the Chinese Academy of Sciences and other institutions recently investigated this pulsar, called PSR J0435+3233, and its. The pulses originating from PSR J0435+3233 arrive approximately 3.2 milliseconds apart, and examining them allows astronomers to track its rotation.

J0435+3233 was found to be in orbit with another type of stellar remnant known as a helium white dwarf. To investigate the system, the researchers reprocessed 192 observing sessions from China's Five-hundred-meter Aperture Spherical radio Telescope, known as FAST, spanning nearly.

Motion toward or away from Earth changes the spacing of arriving pulses via the so-called Doppler effect. If the pulsar accelerates away from Earth, the intervals between pulses become longer, making its rotation appear to slow more rapidly than it actually does.

The broader interest lies in turning an observational clue into something that can be weighed against competing models of the underlying physics. Astrophysics does not have the luxury of controlled experiments; everything is inferred from radiation that traveled across cosmic distances under conditions that cannot be reproduced in a terrestrial laboratory. This makes the interpretation chain longer and more uncertain than in bench science, but it also means that a well-constrained measurement of an extreme object carries theoretical information that no earthbound experiment can provide.

The team also identified gamma-ray pulses in observations from NASA's Fermi satellite. By combining these observations with FAST measurements and information about the visible star, they could reconstruct an elongated outer orbit lasting approximately 73.5 years.
