An international team of scientists has developed a method that can find pulsars in publicly available NASA data about a million times faster than before. This opens the door to discovering many more of these rapidly spinning objects.

Editors have highlighted the following attributes while ensuring the content's credibility: Add to Preferred Sources NASA's Fermi Gamma-ray Space Telescope, illustrated here, scans the entire sky every three hours as it orbits Earth. Using 15 years of data from NASA's Fermi Gamma-ray Space Telescope and a cryptography-based method developed by study co-authors Dotan Gazith and Barak Zackay from the Weizmann Institute of Science in Israel, the team found PSR J1622−7204, an isolated pulsar spinning 261 times.

By comparison, the previous best method failed to find the pulsar, despite using enough computing power that a standard processor would take 600 years to perform the calculation. There's just not enough computing power on Earth to do it. " Traditionally, scientists have searched for these pulsars by breaking Fermi data into smaller chunks and analyzing them by crowdsourcing computing power from volunteers around the world.

We have nothing to do with cryptography in our analysis here, but those algorithms can help us solve the problem of searching for pulsars in our data," Gazith said. These discoveries have long been considered out of reach because a companion star's gravity distorts the signal in ways that have made these pulsars essentially impossible to discover with traditional methods.
