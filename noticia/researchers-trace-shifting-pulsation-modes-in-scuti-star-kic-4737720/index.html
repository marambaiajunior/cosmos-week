Stars in the night sky may look calm and unchanging, but some are in fact constantly "vibrating. " Just as a musical instrument can sound several notes at once, a star can pulse in several modes simultaneously. This article has been reviewed according to Science X's editorial process and policies.

Editors have highlighted the following attributes while ensuring the content's credibility: Add to Preferred Sources The Astrophysical Journal (2026). Dynamic spectrum and TPF pointing correction for KIC 4737720.

Credit: The Astrophysical Journal (2026). A team at the Xinjiang Astronomical Observatory of the Chinese Academy of Sciences has now studied the δ Scuti variable KIC 4737720 using long-term observations from the Kepler and TESS space telescopes.

Long-term monitoring of KIC 4737720 shows that during the Kepler observations one initially dominant mode gradually weakened while another grew stronger and eventually surpassed it. Further analysis traced these changes mainly to the pulsation amplitudes rather than to large shifts in the main frequencies.

The study also identified a set of apparently real "V-shaped frequency drifts. " A closer look at Kepler target-pixel data and pointing-correction records showed that these structures recur on a timescale of about 372 days, very close to Kepler's annual roll period of roughly 372. Jianwei Yao et al, Study of Special Pulsation Modulation Phenomena: A Case Study of the δ Scuti Star KIC 4737720, The Astrophysical Journal (2026).
