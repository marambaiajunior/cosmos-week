A Solar Optical Clock

Precision spectroscopy underpins quantum sensing, atomic timekeeping, and the search for terrestrial-mass exoplanets.

Texto completo

Precision spectroscopy underpins quantum sensing, atomic timekeeping, and the search for terrestrial-mass exoplanets. Finding an Earth-Sun analog requires fractional splitting of spectral features that rivals the precision of the best.

Finding an Earth-Sun analog requires fractional splitting of spectral features that rivals the precision of the best laboratory spectroscopy. This corresponds to resolution of a few parts in $10^5$ of a typical stellar spectral line, or a Doppler velocity precision of less than 10 cm s$^{-1}$.

Only a few astrophysical spectrometers reach this regime, and only by dispersing starlight with large gratings and averaging thousands of lines. Here, we show that comparable precision can be achieved on a single solar line by leveraging techniques used in quantum sensors and optical clocks.

A laser is frequency-locked to the 1565 nm solar Fe I line by coupling to a single mode of the solar thermal field and counted against an optical frequency comb referenced to the SI second, thus creating a solar optical clock. Operating at the fundamental photon-noise limit of thermal heterodyne spectroscopy, we achieve centroid precision of one part in $10^9$ of the optical frequency, or 30 cm s$^{-1}$ in radial velocity.

We recover the Sun's five-minute acoustic oscillations and the expected Rossiter-McLaughlin signal during a solar eclipse. Combining these techniques from quantum sensing with a thirty-meter-class telescope could enable our demonstrated precision on the nearest Sun-like stars in a few hours.

Multiplexing such absolute frequency measurements across lines with different magnetic sensitivity and formation depth would reduce averaging time and help disentangle stellar granulation and activity from the Doppler signature of terrestrial exoplanets, providing new approaches.
