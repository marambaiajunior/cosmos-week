Thanks to the gravitational lensing caused by a massive galaxy cluster and high-resolution images from the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST), astronomers discovered an exploding star when the universe was 4.9 billion years old.

Cutouts are provided for the three images of the SN Ares host galaxy, with the predicted time delays to images 1.1 and 1.2 labeled.

Credit: Conor Larison et al, arXiv (2026). In this study, astronomers led by Conor Larison of the Space Telescope Science Institute report the discovery of a supernova that occurred around 8.8 billion years ago.

They spotted SN Ares in August 2025 in the JWST's near-infrared images taken of the field of the galaxy cluster MACS J0308.9+2645. But their analysis using three lens models revealed that two more images of this supernova are yet to reach us.

Due to the direct relationship between these delays and geometric distance, a measurement of the offset in image arrival times leads to constraints on cosmological parameters such as the local expansion rate of the universe, H0," researchers explain. Researchers say that they cannot predict what the science of the universe will look like in 70 years, but are sure that the reappearance of SN Ares will be a scientifically rich event, worth looking forward to.

Conor Larison et al, SN Ares: A Strongly Lensed Type IIb Supernova at z=1.276 with a Predicted 70-Year Time Delay, arXiv (2026).
