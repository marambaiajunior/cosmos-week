We are consistently launching more and more stuff into space. As of now, space agencies track more than 30, 000 objects larger than a softball, with an expected 1 million more bullet-sized pieces of debris orbiting Earth that are too small.

This article has been reviewed according to Science X's editorial process and policies. Credit: NASA We are consistently launching more and more stuff into space.

As of now, space agencies track more than 30, 000 objects larger than a softball, with an expected 1 million more bullet-sized pieces of debris orbiting Earth that are too small to spot from the ground. Government agencies and private commercial entities are planning to have up to 100, 000 active spacecraft in orbit by the early 2030s, turning some orbital shells into crowded highways.

And according to a new paper by Federico Toson, a researcher at the University of Padova in Italy, available on the arXiv preprint server, the math that regulatory agencies use to determine whether a satellite is "safe" in these highly congested spaces is fundamentally flawed. Standards set by NASA and the European Space Agency rely on color-coded charts that sort missions into one of several buckets based on their risk profile.

But if a mega-constellation operator builds thousands of identical satellites at a cost of $1 million a pop, losing one barely registers on its financial statements. A single Starlink V2 Mini, orbiting at 525 km (326 miles), has a collective risk 317 times higher than the "individual risk. " In a traditional risk assessment, this would mark it as "low" risk, whereas this new framework would place it very clearly in the "very high" camp.

ESA uses an index called the Environmental Consequences of Orbital Breakups (ECOB), and researchers at EPFL in Switzerland have put forward the Space Sustainability Rating.
